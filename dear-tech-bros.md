# Dear Tech Bros: Stop Whining about Wealth Taxes and "Get in the Arena"
## Complaining isn't a policy. Offer a better alternative, or the pitchforks keep coming.

A few weeks ago, I [wrote a piece](https://taxrefactor.substack.com/p/senator-warren-is-right-about-the) arguing that Elizabeth Warren has the right diagnosis (billionaires pay lower effective rates than teachers, "buy/borrow/die" is indefensible) but the wrong prescription: a wealth tax is a new system bolted onto a 75,000-page mess that already doesn't work. Right problem, wrong solution.

Today I want to point a similar critique in a different direction: the indignance that "tech bros" have shown towards various efforts to tax extreme wealth.

Take, for example, the [All-In Podcast](https://www.allinpodcast.co/). When it was first launched, All-In was one of my favorite pods. I appreciated the raw, unfiltered, often contrarian takes on what was happening in the technology markets. Four experienced and outspoken perspectives, different ideologies, stress-testing each other in public. It was a good product.

But somewhere along the way the "first principles" they regularly espoused became "me-first principles." Chamath and Sacks went all in on Trump-era sycophancy, despite [having said after January 6](https://www.thebulwark.com/p/trumps-tech-bro-fanboys-wanted-him) that he should be barred from public office. The show veered hard into politics, and the obvious hypocrisy and political tone-deafness of four very rich guys became overwhelming. So I stopped listening.

But lately, I've caught wind of their latest target of indignation: wealth tax policy. If Senator Warren is offering the wrong answer to extreme wealth inequality, the All-In guys are offering *no* answer. And refusing to propose anything while complaining about everything is, in some ways, the more frustrating posture. It abdicates the chance to actually shape the outcome.

## The whining problem

As All-In's "friend-of-the-pod" [Ray Dalio has repeatedly pointed out](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/big-cycles-over-last-500-years-ray-dalio/), extreme wealth disparity never ends well. His framework, built on 500 years of historical data, identifies wealth concentration at current levels as a leading indicator of internal conflict and political rupture. Not a leftist talking point. Just the data.

Efforts to curb extreme wealth disparity are gaining momentum across the country, with bespoke laws being introduced state by state. In March 2026, Washington state passed [SB 6346](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/howard-schultz-starbucks-ceo-leaving-seattle-washington-millionaire-tax/), a 9.9% income tax on personal earnings over $1M. [Massachusetts passed a 4% millionaire's surtax in 2022](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/4-surtax-on-taxable-income-over-1000000), [Mamdani won in New York](https://www.nytimes.com/article/who-is-zohran-mamdani.html) running (in part) on a pied-à-terre tax and an income surcharge, [Seattle has its JumpStart payroll tax](https://www.seattle.gov/business-license-tax/payroll-expense-tax) on high earners at large companies, and California is now moving toward a [5% wealth tax on net worth over $50M](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/01/29/california-wealth-tax-bill.html).

The All-In crowd's responses to these policies has ranged from:

1. ["You're driving away the job creators."](https://x.com/DavidSacks/status/2005394616020042140) (Sacks: *"Democrats steal everything, then blame job creators for their 'greed.'"*)
2. [Move to Austin / Miami / Nevada, it's great.](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/12/02/tesla-ceo-elon-musk-plans-to-move-to-texas-friends-and-associates-say.html) (Musk to Texas, Lonsdale to Texas, [Page and Brin to Nevada](https://www.foxbusiness.com/politics/google-co-founder-rips-california-billionaire-tax-i-fled-socialism), Schultz to Miami.)
3. [Mamdani is a socialist. So is California.](https://www.foxbusiness.com/politics/google-co-founder-rips-california-billionaire-tax-i-fled-socialism) (Brin: *"I fled socialism with my family in 1979 and know the devastating, oppressive society it created in the Soviet Union. I don't want California to end up in the same place."*)
4. [You can't tax unrealized gains! It's asset seizure!](https://x.com/DavidSacks/status/2004992516303839258) (Sacks, again: *"This is not a tax — this is asset seizure. It's not a one time, it's a first time."*)
5. [We need to cut waste, fraud, and abuse. No taxes needed.](https://x.com/DavidSacks/status/2005394616020042140) (Sacks, again: *"Why does California need a wealth tax? To fund the massive fraud. Red states like Texas and Florida don't even have income taxes."*)
6. [Just unleash AI and the deficit takes care of itself.](https://www.allinpodcast.co/episodes) (Friedberg: *"If we have enough power, the $38T of debt doesn't matter."*)

That is not a "first principles" policy response. It is scattershot complaining devoid of self-awareness.

To steelman: their actual position is not "inequality is fine" or "the deficit isn't a problem." It is closer to "growth solves everything" plus "there's too much fraud and waste." No need for new revenues. Cut taxes, cut regulation, grow the pie, everyone wins. Your slice may shrink as a percentage, but the pie grows so much that your slice is bigger in absolute terms than it ever was. 

The problem with this view is twofold.

First, "the pie gets bigger" is a claim about the *average*. Social stability depends on the *spread*. (Former math teacher here: in a skewed distribution, the mean is the least interesting number.) The median American household has a net worth of [$192,900. The mean is $1,063,700](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf23.pdf), 5.5x higher. When the average and the typical drift that far apart, "the economy is booming" and "I'll never afford a house" are both true. And the second group votes.

This isn't vibes. Raj Chetty and coauthors [found](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22910) that 90% of Americans born in 1940 out-earned their parents, versus 50% of those born in the 1980s. Their punchline: faster GDP growth *alone cannot* bring that back, while spreading growth the way it was spread for the 1940 cohort would reverse more than 70% of the decline. The IMF's Andrew Berg and Jonathan Ostry [found](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2011/09/Berg.htm) that inequality predicts when growth spells *end*: closing half the inequality gap between Latin America and emerging Asia would more than double the expected length of a growth spell. And Alberto Alesina and Roberto Perotti, [studying 70 countries from 1960 to 1985](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4486), traced the mechanism: inequality breeds discontent, discontent breeds political instability, and instability kills investment. In other words, the spread eventually comes for the growth. (The causal evidence is messier than either side admits, but the pattern keeps showing up.)

Second, growth alone cannot close a deficit gap this large. [CBO's long-term projections](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61187) consistently show structural deficits even under optimistic productivity assumptions. Growth and cost cutting are both necessary. They are not sufficient. Revenue will need to be part of the answer. (More on the math of deficits in a future post.)

You can dismiss any one of these state-level policies as a local fluke. You cannot dismiss all of them. There is a political groundswell gaining momentum around wealth disparity, as there has been many times before in history. And when the tax-the-rich movement goes federal (and I think it will), the pied-à-terre in Miami doesn't help. Howard Schultz would have to move to Dubai instead of Florida. That is a much harder sell.

And here's the irony. Your investment thesis, repeated basically every episode, is that AI unleashes unprecedented growth. If you're right, wealth concentrates faster than it ever has (frontier models are a multi-billion-dollar fixed cost only a handful of companies can absorb), in a country where [52% of Americans are already more concerned than excited about AI](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/08/28/growing-public-concern-about-the-role-of-artificial-intelligence-in-daily-life/). That isn't a recipe for a quiet wealth tax. It's a recipe for pitchforks. If you believe your own thesis, you should be the loudest voices in America for fixing the tax code *now*.

So rather than just complaining, how about a) recognizing the social risks of wealth disparity and a runaway federal deficit, and b) actually proposing some viable, systems-oriented "first principles" alternatives to these hacky, one-off state tax policies?

## First principles, actually

So basically the All-In response to taxing wealth is "we earned it, leave us alone, let us cook." Here's why that frame leaves out some load-bearing facts.

**1. You earned it. Mostly.** You'll say you built this, and mostly, you did. But the internet came out of [DARPA-funded research](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/6323/funding-a-revolution-government-support-for-computing-research), your chips came out of Bell Labs and the [$52B CHIPS Act](https://www.commerce.gov/news/blog/2022/09/one-year-chips-and-science-act), and luck did more than you think. A [2018 simulation by Pluchino, Biondo, and Rapisarda](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07068) gave 1,000 agents normally-distributed talent and random luck. The most successful agent had a talent score of 0.61 (the mean was 0.6). The most talented agent, at 0.89, ended up below average in success. Talent is necessary. Luck decides the tail. That's not an insult. It's just a reason the system that made you rich needs maintenance.

**2. Wealth begets wealth.** Once you are already rich, your next venture has a massive advantage over the kid in the dorm room with the same idea. You take less risk. You stay rich if it fails. You can self-fund. You can absorb losses. You can wait out bad markets. You have the network. The current tax code amplifies this advantage (step-up basis, the [buy/borrow/die playbook](https://www.propublica.org/article/the-secret-irs-files-trove-of-never-before-seen-records-reveal-how-the-wealthiest-avoid-income-tax), perpetually lower capital gains rates than ordinary income). If you actually want a meritocracy of ideas, advocate for one.

**3. Pay attention to history.** As noted, Ray Dalio wrote *the* book on this: [*Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order*](https://www.principles.com/the-changing-world-order/). His framework actually identifies two parallel risks: extreme wealth concentration and unsustainable debt. Both are at historic levels right now. Both are addressable through the same set of structural tax reforms. (More on that in a future post.) Extreme concentration ends the same way every time: 1789, 1917, the 1930s. The U.S. avoided that fate during the Depression by passing the New Deal, which is to say, by reducing concentration through policy. This is not a leftist talking point. This is pattern recognition from the guy you keep quoting.

## Some people are actually getting in the arena

And in case you think nobody from your demographic ever does this, let me name a few extremely wealthy people who have, to use Chamath's phrase, "gotten in the arena."

In [December 2025, Mitt Romney wrote a New York Times op-ed](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/19/opinion/romney-tax-the-rich.html) titled, in essence, "tax me more, I was wrong before." He called the loopholes "tax caverns." He named carried interest, the FICA cap, real estate depreciation, and the stepped-up basis on inheritances over $100M as specific things to fix. He used Elon Musk as an example of how the current system breaks. He is a former Republican presidential nominee and former Bain Capital founder, and he put his name on a concrete list. The [WSJ editorial board attacked him for it](https://thehill.com/homenews/media/5659642-wsj-romney-tax-rich-criticism/). That is what taking a real position looks like.

Mark Cuban, who paid $275.9M in taxes in 2023 and was [proudly public about it](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/04/15/how-much-mark-cuban-pays-in-taxes.html), has been actively advising campaigns on capital gains policy. He talked Harris down from Biden's higher cap gains proposal because he can articulate why higher rates discourage investment in early-stage, riskier companies (especially those started by women, people of color, and recent grads). He disagrees with Warren on unrealized gains. He thinks billionaires should pay more under the current realization-based system. That is a coherent position with specifics. You can disagree with it. It is in the arena.

Bill Gates and Warren Buffett have been [publicly arguing for years](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2024ltr.pdf) that people like them should pay more. Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway paid a record $26.8B in federal taxes in 2024. The "Buffett Rule" was a specific proposal with a number attached.

You do not have to agree with any of these people. But they made proposals. They put their names on something concrete. They got attacked by their own side and held their position. *That* is what "getting in the arena" looks like.

## Your move

I would love to hear an episode where you engage seriously with tax reform and make concrete proposals. Not "Mamdani is a socialist" or "Warren is a hypocrite" or "Come join me in Austin," but rather a 30-minute thought experiment about tax reform that would actually work.

I'll even give you a starting point. The [Lifetime Gains Framework](https://taxrefactor.substack.com/p/the-lifetime-gains-framework) is a more elegant alternative to a wealth tax: no new tax type, no annual valuations, just closing the exits (stepped-up basis, buy/borrow/die) on the system we already have. Yes, it would cost some of you money. Happy to walk any of you through it. (Chamath, my DMs are open.)

The wealth tax movement is going to keep gaining momentum. Societies simply will not tolerate our current levels of wealth disparity forever (see Dalio, once again). So acknowledge the diagnosis. Stop defending bolt-ons. Propose systemic fixes. Get specific. Better to negotiate now, while you still have leverage, than to have a worse version forced on you later. The pitchforks aren't going away.

Get in the arena, guys.
